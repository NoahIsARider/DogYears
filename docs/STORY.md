# DogYears: the small house

This screenplay follows seven days in a shared home after Ellis has died. The player is unnamed. Ellis uses they/them pronouns. Mara is a living friend who knows the house and its ordinary objects. No actor or character model appears. No cause of death is disclosed: the story does not promise a mystery, a reconstruction of an accident, or a chance to undo it.

The relationship is carried by specific habits. Ellis drinks coffee cold, wears a blue shirt before its cuffs are dry, steps into shoes without undoing the laces, and leaves the good cup for the player. The player wants a clear kitchen table, dislikes the open bedroom window, and can recognise Ellis’s shirt without looking. The last morning includes annoyance, a practical request and a laugh. None is presented as the sentence that caused or could have prevented the loss.

Ellis has been making a small model of their current house. They began it when the pair talked casually about moving. The model preserves the door that catches in its frame. The player had thought this meant Ellis might finally mend the real door. Instead, Ellis was making the particular house small enough to take somewhere else. The roof is removable so light can reach inside. This meaning is not given in an opening monologue; it emerges from a reversed calendar pattern, a shaving in a shirt cuff, and a small door that catches like its full-sized counterpart.

The recurring phrase is “Hold this end.” In its first appearances it is simply how two people work together. On day five the player tries to fit the final roof beam. They can support it, but they cannot recreate the exact pause before Ellis used to say “Got it.” A clamp or paper brace would solve the carpentry. The game must make that clear. This is not an impossible puzzle in which the dead person was the only competent helper. What the player discovers is that fixing the object was not the same desire as hearing the answer again.

The emotional turn comes through a particular request. The player photographs the pin holes and asks Mara to hold the beam. Mara does not say that everything will be all right. She agrees, asks which end and brings the clamp anyway. The player moves the bowl out of the doorway and sets out two cups for two living people. Ellis’s place is not overwritten; it becomes possible to remember their preference while setting a different cup for a visitor.

On the last day the player takes their own key, lets the front latch close and holds the back door for Mara. The sound of a key, established as a false expectation in the first scene, becomes an ordinary sound caused by the player’s own action. Mara stays outside the frame. A bag lowered onto boards, a second steady shadow, and an outside voice saying “Which end?” provide enough presence. The answer to the day-five pause is “Got it,” spoken by someone else, without suggesting that someone else is Ellis.

## First outline and criticism

The first outline used seven object visits, each followed by a thought about loss. It had a clear theme but little dramatic movement. Most objects delivered the same information: a familiar action now has no reply. The last-day message arrived as an externally supplied resolution. Nothing the player did caused the relationship with the living friend to change.

A second weakness was the risk of treating the unfinished model as a moral obligation. “Finish their work” can sound like a grief achievement, while leaving it unfinished can sound like failure. A third weakness was using the shirt or letter as a hidden farewell. That device would turn ordinary domestic objects into clues and make the dead person seem to have anticipated the game.

The revision therefore gives the same objects different roles on later days. The cup first reproduces an automatic routine, later holds the memory of a small argument, and finally becomes preparation for a visitor. The shirt first gives a tactile mismatch, then a shaving that connects intimacy to the miniature. The letter first refuses to be a farewell, then reveals incomplete measurements that the player has been treating as instructions. The roof project becomes a shared gesture that cannot be repeated exactly, but can be continued differently.

The visitor’s arrival is also earned in stages. Mara leaves bread without demanding a reply, explains how to ask for more without speaking, returns the lid of a familiar tin, offers a borrowed clamp, and offers to do one ordinary thing together. The player’s eventual request answers these offers. Mara is persistent enough to be credible and restrained enough not to become a therapeutic speech generator.

## Performance and interface

The data in `src/story.js` is the playable script. Twenty required scenes share eight keepsake anchors across seven chapters. An individual visit is written for roughly seventy to one hundred seconds when experienced without rushing. This includes walking a few steps, looking at a second target, allowing a sound to settle and performing two or three short actions. It never means holding E continuously for ninety seconds. Actions last six to ten seconds. Their beats are distributed over progress but should also permit a pause between actions. Quoted dialogue is presented as remembered or present-tense caption fragments in the current build, not a claim of recorded voice performances. Sound direction refers to environmental cues; actor recordings remain a separate production step.

Visible text stays short. A memory begins with one thought. Later beats are usually under twenty words and should replace, not accumulate underneath, the previous sentence. Task labels are physical verbs. The archive, developer notes and chapter explanations never appear as a panel in the game. The script should leave enough time between lines that the player can notice the object before the next line interprets it.

Narrative ink now uses a bundled bold handwritten face in charcoal, with a restrained warm edge for dark backgrounds. Lines sit beside their physical anchors with parallax and depth occlusion. Chapter cards finish fading before the opening thought begins. Interface controls remain brief; no progress bar interprets the player's physical action as a task score.

The player can leave a memory. Early departure is not failure and should not cause a guilt line. For chapter pacing, record a meaningful visit after the first physical action or key authored beat, rather than after merely entering the effect. Completing every gesture may offer a fuller silence or an extra detail; it must not be scored as being better at grief. Daily access checks depend on scene keys, never possession or disposal. If the player has put away a pillow, the mattress seam and folded case remain valid anchors. If they have released the calendar, its detached pattern can remain in a drawer. Do not respawn disposed keepsakes simply because the script mentions them.

The counter is still a time pressure, but it is not the story’s verdict. It can change how long the light appears to hold, how the mirror is perceived and which ending is offered. It does not determine whether a remembered act of kindness happened. Optional discoveries grant neither points nor chapter completion.

## Chapter direction

### One: before the light changes

The player begins with their hand’s knowledge of the house. A strap is worn where Ellis held it. A latch is left loose for occupied hands. Two cups settle into two old marks in the counter. Warmth is confined to a narrow path of late sunlight rather than an inviting whole-room wash. The first apparent return is only a cooling radiator. Leave the second chair slightly pulled out. The important action is setting down the second cup, not deciding what to do with it.

### Two: the space beside you

The bed, shirt and calendar reveal unequal habits. A pillow warms under the player’s hand because a living hand is there. A caught sleeve is freed without filling the shirt with a body. A circled Saturday remains unexplained. Resist giving every absence an answer. A slight opening at the window should move one cuff and one curtain edge; theatrical wind would impose a mood the objects already carry.

### Three: an ordinary morning

The player recalls wanting the table cleared. Ellis moved the plans rather than winning or losing the argument. The unfinished letter is not a farewell; beneath it is a list containing milk, a bulb and brass pins. At the entrance, a flattened shoe heel recalls a familiar impatience. The last exchange includes a laugh. It is not rewritten into a perfect goodbye. Its incompleteness is what makes it a believable final morning.

### Four: small enough to carry

Three small links provide the recontextualisation. The reverse of a calendar page contains the roof pattern. A wood shaving is caught in the blue cuff. The miniature door catches in its frame. Only then does the player understand why Ellis measured the crooked part instead of repairing it. Light must reach the miniature floor when its roof is lifted. That physical image does more work than an explanatory voice-over about carrying home within oneself.

### Five: hold this end

This chapter rises, breaks and rests. The plan offers partial measurements, so the player tries the actual beam. The first attempt feels feasible. The beam tilts slightly while the pin is aligned. The pin rolls only a few centimetres and remains visible. The task ends by resting the beam on folded paper. No breakage, loud crash, reset or wrong-answer animation follows. At the bedside the player stops trying to reach an emotional outcome by completing an object. Room tone continues. Leave an actual interval before Mara’s next offer arrives.

### Six: one small thing

The player makes a paper support, photographs the open roof and sends a practical request. The support is competent and temporary. The message is vulnerable without being a confession demanded by an interface. Then the player clears the threshold and prepares cups. The absent person is still allowed to be remembered at the table. A different cup for Mara expresses difference rather than substitution. Do not finish the model automatically after the message is sent.

### Seven: the other side

The player makes only their side of the bed and leaves a useful object ready for tonight. The future is first made concrete as somewhere to sleep. They take one key rather than gathering every key as symbolic loot. The final role is holding a real door while another person sets a bag down. Leave the miniature imperfect or briefly offscreen. The climax answers a gesture, not a production checklist. After the player lets go, time can reach evening.

## Optional discoveries

Twenty-four small observations support the house without explaining the plot. A different screw in the reading glasses shows an old repair. A dish bought for olives became a key dish. A felt pad missing from a chair produced a recognisable scrape. A receipt in a loose window came from a place that has since closed. The price tag on a lampshade protected a pale rectangle from sunlight. These are not evidence of how Ellis died. Their value is that people lived a long, untidy, ordinary life before the game’s seven days began.

The discoveries are authored as single sentences and should be placed at physical inspection distance. Their titles help identify a thing; they do not belong in a checklist. After the thought fades, the player can remain, turn the object or continue walking. New meaning can come from seeing a familiar object again after a chapter without triggering any new interface.

## Endings and restraint

Keep all five ending conditions available, but avoid making the lowest counter the only emotionally legitimate conclusion. An endless afternoon is a suspension, not a punishment screen. Sitting on the other side of a door can be a meaningful partial change. Leaving with a phone permits a living reply without requiring every object to be disposed of. The fully released ending concerns time becoming ordinary, not proof that attachment was wrong. The hidden Laika association should remain about departure and uncertainty; it must not imply that the player ought to disappear or that being sent away is the correct resolution of bereavement.

For the ordinary ending, do not require a final motivational monologue after “Got it.” The bag, the clamp, two cups and the unfinished roof already establish what can happen next. The player should be able to continue looking at the afternoon for a while. Only then should an unobtrusive ending title appear.
